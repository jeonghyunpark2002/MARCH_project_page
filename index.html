Abstract

Real-world multi-hop QA is naturally linked with ambiguity, where a single query can trigger multiple reasoning paths that require independent resolution. Since ambiguity can occur at any stage, models must navigate layered uncertainty throughout the entire reasoning chain.

Despite its prevalence in real-world user queries, previous benchmarks have primarily focused on single-hop ambiguity, leaving the complex interaction between multi-step inference and layered ambiguity underexplored. In this paper, we introduce MARCH, a benchmark for their intersection, with 2,209 multi-hop ambiguous questions curated via multi-LLM verification and validated by human annotation with strong agreement.

Our experiments reveal that even state-of-the-art models struggle with MARCH, confirming that combining ambiguity resolution with multi-step reasoning is a significant challenge. To address this, we propose CLARION, a two-stage agentic framework that explicitly decouples ambiguity planning from evidence-driven reasoning, significantly outperforms existing approaches, and paves the way for robust reasoning systems.

🎯 Motivation

The Challenge of Multi-hop Ambiguous QA

Multi-hop Question Answering presents a significant reasoning challenge, requiring models to construct logical chains by connecting disparate pieces of information. The intersection of multi-hop reasoning and ambiguity creates a uniquely difficult setting where uncertainty scales exponentially.

In multi-hop ambiguous QA, ambiguity can emerge at any step of the reasoning chain, often remaining latent until prior steps are resolved.

🔷 Semantic Ambiguity Interpret

Homonyms/aliases or entity-name collisions (one name → multiple entities) yielding disjoint evidence trails. Choosing the wrong entity invalidates downstream hops.

Example: "Mustang" → Ford (car) vs. Fender (guitar)

🔶 Syntactic Ambiguity Resolve

Multiple valid parses induce different inter-hop dependencies, changing which intermediate evidence is needed. Cues: pronouns, PP attachment, coordination.

Example: Instrumental vs. Attributive reading of telescope question

🟢 Constraint Ambiguity Generalize

An over-specific modifier is unnecessary or mismatched with evidence granularity, causing a valid chain to be pruned early. Relaxing the constraint recovers the path.

Example: "highest mountain in Europe" → Mont Blanc vs. Elbrus

Analysis of real-world user queries from the lmsys-chat-1m corpus reveals that 48.4% of questions are ambiguous, 17.7% involve multi-hop reasoning, and 13.3% overlap—yet no benchmark specifically targets this intersection.

📊 MARCH: A Benchmark for Multi-Hop Ambiguous QA

We introduce MARCH (Multi-hop Ambiguity Reasoning CHain), a benchmark with 2,209 multi-hop ambiguous questions derived from MuSiQue. Each example is paired with type-specific clarified questions, interpretation-grounded short answers, supporting evidence passages, and a synthesized long answer.

① Ambiguity Detection & Clarification

Four LLMs (GPT-4.1, Llama-4-Maverick, Qwen3-235B, Claude-Sonnet-4) vote with full-agreement rule—a type label is assigned only when all four detectors agree, reducing single-model bias.

② Document Collection

Clarified questions are decomposed into atomic sub-questions, retrieved from English Wikipedia, and re-ranked with Qwen3-8B-Embedding for evidence aligned with each interpretation.

③ Answer Generation

GPT-4.1 produces per-interpretation short answers and a synthesized long answer that connects all interpretations into a coherent statement.

④ Filtering & Quality Control

Three LLMs independently verify alignment. Only unanimous cases are retained. Validated by 5 human annotators with Fleiss' κ up to 0.95.

✅ Human Validation: Stratified sample of 60 instances (20 per type), annotated by 5 experts. Over 90% long-answer validity (all interpretations integrated) and strong inter-annotator agreement: Ambiguity detection κ = 0.92 · Clarification quality κ = 0.89 · Answer consistency κ = 0.95

🧠 CLARION: Agentic Framework

We propose CLARION (CLarifying Ambiguity with a Reasoning and InstructiON), a two-stage agentic framework that explicitly decouples ambiguity planning from evidence retrieval.

Standard retrieval methods typically fail by committing to a single dominant interpretation, thereby pruning alternative paths. CLARION overcomes this by separating Planning from Acting.

🗺️ Planning Agent

  1. Ambiguity Detection — determines if the question is ambiguous; if not, passes directly to Acting Agent
  2. Type Classification — categorizes into Syntactic, Constraint, or Semantic
  3. Question Clarification — rewrites into clarified variants that resolve ambiguity while preserving the information need

⚡ Acting Agent (ReAct Loop)

Executes a Thought → Action → Observation loop for each clarified interpretation. Three possible actions:

  • Search — retrieve external documents
  • Planning — self-correction: refine/expand the interpretation set when evidence inconsistencies are observed
  • Answer — synthesize final output covering all interpretations

Isolating Multi-hop and Ambiguity Effects

Modern RAG and agentic baselines perform reasonably well on single-hop ambiguity (ASQA) and standard multi-hop reasoning (MuSiQue) in isolation. However, this success does not transfer to MARCH, where ambiguity is latent and path-dependent. Early interpretation choices fix bridge entities, steer downstream retrieval, and prune alternative branches — causing cascading errors across hops.

🔍 Why is MARCH Hard?

The failure of existing methods is largely path-dependent: committing to an early interpretation conditions downstream decomposition and retrieval. Each hop's output serves as the input to the next — a misresolved ambiguity at hop 1 fixes an incorrect bridge entity, which steers hop 2 retrieval toward an irrelevant trajectory, and so on.

Standard RAG-based baselines retrieve with the ambiguous question, over-focusing on the most frequent interpretation and under-covering evidence for other branches — especially when different interpretations induce disjoint bridge entities and documents.
